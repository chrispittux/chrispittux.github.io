The Challenge

The app only knew the past. Customers needed the next two weeks.

The bank came to Capco with an app that reported the past. Account numbers, balances, history. Nothing about what was coming. Upcoming bills, shortfalls, savings plans: for all of that, customers still leaned on calls, lines, and memory. I knew, because I used to be the person they called.

Research

Years across the teller line, turned into evidence.

I came in with hundreds of customer conversations already in my head. That's a head start, not a finding. My old bank was a different bank with different customers, so I treated the teller years as hypotheses and built four ways to check them against each other.

"It says it on the screen, but I can't find it."

01

Recorded calls with permission, post-call confirmations, and a research report after every round. Requirements couldn't drift, and nobody's boss got surprised in a dev review.

02

A heuristic evaluation, plus conversations with the people who built it, so I understood not just what was there but why.

The island's P2P culture, PNC's Money Bar. Proof the patterns work, not just my taste.

04

The teller years

Hypotheses from the floor, then checked in the room. Some survived. Some didn't. That's the point.

The reframe that drove the design: customers didn't need a better report of what their money did. They needed the app to manage what happens next.

Design

Forecast, don't report.

Home. Customers opened the app and hunted. Home had to answer "how much do I have, and what needs me" without a single tap. We put the due date on the card itself instead of in a notification center, because warnings you find late are warnings that failed. The four actions people actually come for, deposit, transfer, withdraw, pay bills, live above the fold instead of behind a menu.

Home, accounts tab. Balances, the four core actions, and the P2P feed in one scroll.
Home, cards tab. The due date sits on the card it belongs to, not in a notification center.

Accounts. Framed around the questions customers actually call the bank about. Why are my two balances different: answered inline instead of an FAQ link, because the confusion happens at the number. How close am I to my limit: a bar instead of a number, because the bar is a feeling. What do I owe, and when: due date, statement, minimum, and the pay button in one card, because the decision and the action belong together.

"Why are my two balances different?" Available versus current, answered in plain language right where the confusion happens.
"How close am I to my limit?" Credit drawn as a bar, with everything needed to pay in one card.

The forecast. Every app shows what money did. Nobody showed customers what money was about to do. The first pass had all the data and none of the answers: no projected balance, negatives blending in, nowhere to act. What shipped projects every bill and paycheck forward with a running balance, because "can I make rent" is the real question, a legend that reads like a sentence, and the fix inside the warning itself, because a warning without a next step is just anxiety.

The first pass, recreated. All the data, none of the answers. The car payment that overdraws you looks exactly like rent, and the fix lived three screens away. The original stayed with the client, so this is a faithful rebuild.
The running balance. What's left after each event clears, not just what each one costs.
The calendar view. The same forecast, scannable by week, with the legend that reads like a sentence.
The fix inside the warning. The projected shortfall names the exact amount and hands you the transfer that solves it, on the same card. A warning without a next step is just anxiety.

Engagement, defined. The client wanted their P2P feature front and center everywhere. Users didn't. Balancing that was the job: the feed keeps its real estate on the home screen, where it earns it, because on the island P2P is how money moves, without being wallpapered across every surface. Personalization became progress you can feel: goal rings that say "almost there" before any number does, and one stat turned into a nudge.

Progress you can feel. Goal rings say "almost there" before any number does. 89% hits different than $1,787.
A stat turned into a nudge. Social proof that prompts action instead of a chart that prompts nothing.
Results

Shipped. And how I'd measure it.

Shipped on iOS and Android: the full consumer surface. Home, accounts, forecasting, insights, goals, and the bank's P2P feed, launched to the public as part of a staged marketing rollout.

I rolled off at delivery, before public launch metrics, so I won't dress that up. What I left behind was the instrument panel I'd read: calendar engagement, taps on the shortfall fix, overdraft incidents against baseline, and P2P return visits.

Real work, real constraints: if I can't back it with a number, it's not on this page.

Reflection

What I'd carry into the next one. And what I'd trust less.

My banking years bought speed and stakeholder trust, and they're also the thing I'd trust less next time: real customers should see the calendar before it ships, not just stakeholders. The rest holds. Every call on this page traces back to a session, an audit finding, or a pattern proven somewhere else first.

I'll always start with instinct. I just don't ship on it.
